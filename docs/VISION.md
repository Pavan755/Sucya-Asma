# Vision and scope

## Vision

Sucya-Asma turns carefully researched stories into clear, emotionally engaging animated films and short-form releases. It begins with a small Ramayana-inspired series grounded in texts and living storytelling traditions. Each production should show viewers what kind of source a story comes from and preserve a trail from source to claim, script, shot, asset, and final edit.

## What the project is

- A repeatable story-to-screen workflow, supported by reusable tools and narrowly scoped AI agents.
- A character-led animated series, built from short episodes with consistent characters and places.
- A learning project: decisions, scripts, prompts, notebooks, and code remain understandable and versioned.
- A human-directed studio. Automation drafts, organizes, transforms and checks; the creative lead decides what is supported, respectful, and ready to release.

## What “the brain learns” means here

The system does not train itself on books or gain verified understanding merely by ingesting them. It builds a cited, searchable project knowledge base: bibliographic records, lawful notes, claim/source links, character and environment canon, and approved episode decisions. Agents retrieve that material to draft work. A human checks each citation and interpretation. Model training or fine-tuning is a separate future decision requiring rights, data, compute, and evaluation plans.

## Initial scope

Start with one short pilot from the existing Ramayana-associated story folder. Build the source ledger and character references; produce a storyboard and rough cut using stills, maps, narration, and simple motion graphics. Introduce generated video only for shots where it improves the story and can maintain quality and continuity. After the pilot works, make a small batch of episodes with the same production system.

## Story and source categories

Each episode must name its source category:

1. **Textual account:** a specific text, edition/translation, section/page/verse, and interpretive limits.
2. **Historical account:** claims supported by inspectable historical evidence and qualified scholarship.
3. **Traditional narrative:** a story preserved in oral, devotional, regional, or community tradition, with variants identified where possible.
4. **Adaptation or dramatization:** a creative retelling that identifies invention, compression, combination, or inference.

Categories may overlap, but never imply that one proves another. Distinguish epic tradition and its texts from claims about archaeological or historical events. Do not call a story verified without citations and a stated method.

## Audience and release

Choose the initial audience and language before locking the pilot script. Design each episode as a complete story first; then derive trailers, vertical clips, stills, captions, and posts from approved material. Social versions must retain enough context to stay accurate. Release plans, credits, rights checks, captions, thumbnails, descriptions, and archive copies are part of production.

## Success for the first pilot

- One audience and central question are clear.
- Every central claim can be traced to a source record.
- Character appearance, voice, and motion match an approved bible.
- The storyboard shows where evidence, interpretation, and dramatization appear.
- The project can be reopened and its media relinked.
- A clean master, captions, source/rights records, and a small social package are delivered.
- A retrospective identifies what to automate next.
